# Customer Purchase Prediction Project: Complete Guide

A personal project guide: model, notebook and Marketing presentation.

## What this project is

I built a model that predicts which bank customers are going to buy a product, and I present it to someone in Marketing. The model reaches an AUC of 0.84 and makes it possible to contact 30% fewer customers while earning more money than contacting everyone.

This guide explains everything in the notebook and the presentation, so I can present it confidently and answer questions.

**What the presentation covers, in this order:**

1. Universe and target
2. Process
3. Model performance
4. Feature importance and analysis of the top variables
5. Hyperparameters and final model
6. Conclusions
7. Extra: a campaign simulation

It is aimed at a Marketing audience: fewer formulas, more "what the business gains."

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning | In this project |
|---|---|---|
| Universe | The group of customers being analyzed | 23,191 customers |
| Target (TGT) | What we want to predict: 1 = bought, 0 = didn't buy | Purchase in March or April 2019 |
| ABT | Analytic Base Table: one row per customer with all their variables | 23,191 rows × 685 variables |
| Variable (feature) | A data point describing the customer | Balance, salary, card spending |
| Train / test | The model learns on train (70%) and is evaluated on test (30%), customers it has never seen | 16,233 / 6,958 customers |
| LightGBM | The algorithm: builds many small decision trees, each correcting the errors of the previous one | The final model |
| Score | The purchase probability the model assigns to each customer, from 0 to 1 | E.g. 0.75 = 75% chance |
| Decile | Customers sorted by score and split into 10 equal groups. Decile 1 = the 10% most likely to buy | 10 groups of ~2,300 customers |
| AUC | How well the model ranks. The probability that a random buyer has a higher score than a random non-buyer. 0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect | 0.84 on test |
| Lift | How many times better than random selection. Lift 2 = that group buys twice the average | 2.6 in decile 1 |
| Overfitting | The model "memorizes" train and performs poorly on new customers. Detected when train AUC is much higher than test AUC | 0.85 vs 0.84: none |
| Feature importance | How much each variable contributes to the predictions | Active credit card = 29% |
| Hyperparameters | The model's "knobs," set before training (number of trees, depth, etc.) | Chosen with random search |
| Cross-validation | Split train into 3 parts, train on 2 and evaluate on the third, rotating. Used to make choices without touching test | 3-fold |
| Data leakage | The model uses data it wouldn't really have at prediction time. Inflates results | Why target % is recalculated using train only |
| Outlier | An extreme value, far from the rest | Multi-million balances vs. a median of $1,100 |
| 99th percentile (p99) | The value that leaves 99% of the data below it | Cap for monetary amounts |

## 1. Universe and target

I analyzed 23,191 customers, and 29.4% of them (6,823) bought the product in March or April 2019.

**The data.** `data.csv` has one row per customer per month, from August 2018 to April 2019, with about 26,500 customers per month and 77 columns (balances, salary, card, operations by channel, insurance, age, region, etc.).

**Time windows.** The past is used to predict the future:

| Window | Months | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| History | August 2018 to January 2019 (6 months) | All customer variables come from here |
| Gap | February 2019 | Not used. Simulates the time the bank needs to build and launch a campaign |
| Target | March and April 2019 | If the customer bought in either month, TGT = 1 |

**Universe filters.** Customers present in the database all 9 months (26,483), who in January 2019 have no product bundle and no co-branded card. That leaves 23,191.

*Why these filters:* I want customers with complete history to describe them, and who don't already hold products similar to or including the one being sold.

**Watch out** for the dataset's own Target column: it has the same value in every month for a given customer (if they buy in March, it already shows 1 in January). So it is never used as a variable, since that would hand the model the answer.

## 2. Process: data preparation (Part 1 of the notebook)

The raw data was turned into a table with one row per customer and 685 variables (the ABT).

**Nulls (only 3 variables had them)**

- *Average balance (4 records):* filled with the average of the first-day and last-day balance of the month.
- *Card type:* empty throughout the history, only populated in March and April. Taken from March. If the customer had no active card in January, it is "No card"; if they had a card but no data, the most frequent type.
- *Region:* same, taken from March or April. Each customer has a single region, so it is a fixed attribute.

**Outliers.** Four monetary variables had very long tails (a few customers with huge amounts): average balance, salary, card spending and card limit. They were capped at the 99th percentile: anything above that value is replaced by it. No customer is removed and the ordering is preserved. I chose p99 because it is the least aggressive (it only touches the top 1%); p95 trims too much, and "3 standard deviations" relies on a deviation inflated by the very outliers it is meant to catch.

**ABT: three types of variables**

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | The January 2019 snapshot value as-is | Average balance, card limit, number of investments |
| Transform | Values computed with some logic | Customer tenure, months since last product, number of active products, Yes/No converted to 1/0 |
| Aggregate | Summaries of the history for 30 variables (savings account, channels, card) | Sum, max, min, mean, median, std dev over the last 6 and 3 months; change January vs. November and January vs. August |

Aggregates are the majority: 30 variables × (8 summaries × 2 windows + 6 changes) = 660.

**Categorical variables (text converted to numbers)**

| Variable | Treatment | What was observed |
|---|---|---|
| Card type | Cards with fewer than 300 customers grouped into "Other," then target % encoding | No card buys 6%; card J55660104XX012 buys 46% |
| Region | Target % | Small differences: from 24% (Center) to 34% (Patagonia) |
| Age range | Ordinal (preserves order) + 3 groups + target % | Purchase rate rises with age |
| Sex | One-hot encoding (0/1) | Small difference between men and women |

*Target % encoding = replacing each category with the percentage of buyers it has. E.g. "No card" becomes 6.*

**Final cleanup:** blanks from division by zero were filled with 0 and 7 constant variables were dropped (the insurance products: nobody in the universe has them). Result: an ABT of 23,191 customers × 685 variables.

## 3. Model, hyperparameters and final model (Part 2 of the notebook)

Of the 685 variables, the final model uses 15, with a LightGBM whose hyperparameters were chosen by testing 50 combinations.

**The steps, in order**

1. **Split train and test (70/30).** Stratified: both parts have the same purchase rate (29.4%). Test is kept for the end and not used for any decision.
2. **Recalculate target % using train only.** If it were computed on everyone, the variable would "know" the outcome of test customers (data leakage). Values change by about 2 points at most.
3. **Remove correlated variables.** If two variables have a correlation above 0.80, only one is kept. 462 are dropped and 223 remain.
4. **First model with the 223 and an importance ranking.** Then I test keeping the top 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 and 50, compared with cross-validation inside train.
5. **Choose 15 variables.** With 15, AUC is 0.834; with 30 it is 0.842: under 0.01 improvement in exchange for doubling what must be explained and maintained. If I prefer 30, it changes in a single line (`N_VARIABLES`).
6. **Hyperparameter search with RandomizedSearchCV:** tries 50 random combinations, each evaluated with 3-fold cross-validation on train. Metric: AUC.

**Chosen final model (cross-validated AUC: 0.836)**

| Hyperparameter | Value | What it controls |
|---|---|---|
| n_estimators | 200 | Number of trees |
| learning_rate | 0.05 | How much each tree learns. Lower = more cautious |
| max_depth | 4 | Maximum depth of each tree |
| num_leaves | 22 | Maximum number of leaves per tree |
| min_child_samples | 200 | Minimum customers per leaf. Prevents rules for tiny groups |
| subsample | 0.8 | Each tree sees 80% of customers |
| colsample_bytree | 0.7 | Each tree sees 70% of variables |
| reg_lambda | 10 | Penalty that restrains the model to prevent overfitting |

The overall idea: shallow trees, leaves with many customers and regularization, all aimed at a model that generalizes well rather than memorizing train.

**Methodological choice:** I don't use test for early stopping (halting training when it stops improving). Test is untouched until the end, so the evaluation is honest. I also choose the number of variables with cross-validation instead of fixing it at 10.

## 4. Model performance

The model ranks well and is stable: AUC 0.84 on customers it has never seen, and the top 10% by score buys 2.6 times more than average.

**AUC:** train 0.854, test 0.836. The gap is small (0.02), so there is no overfitting.

**Deciles.** Decile cutoffs are calculated on train and applied to test, to measure how it would work with new customers (see the test decile table in the notebook: 6,958 customers).

The drop is smooth from decile to decile: the model doesn't just find the best customers, it ranks the whole base. Deciles 8, 9 and 10 buy under 5%.

**Gain curve (easiest to explain to Marketing):** contacting the top 30% of the base by score finds 62% of buyers; with 50%, it finds 86%. At random, 30% of contacts would find 30%.

**Confusion matrix.** With the default cutoff (score above 0.5), 22% of the base is contacted, 66% of those contacted buy, and 50% of buyers are found. Lowering the cutoff to 0.3 finds 81% of buyers, but contacts 45% of the base. For Marketing it is clearer to talk in deciles than in cutoffs.

**Rule of thumb for lift:** 2 is good, 2.5 is quite good, 3 is excellent. Mine is 2.6 in decile 1.

## 5. Feature importance and customer profile

Having an active credit card is by far the most important factor (29% of importance), followed by how card spending has evolved and how much the savings account is used.

**Gain vs. split.** Gain measures how much the model improves each time it uses the variable (the most interpretable, and the one I show). Split counts how many times the trees use it to make a cut.

| # | Variable | What it measures | % gain | Purchase rate by variable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | CreditCard_Active | Has an active credit card | 29.3 | With card 42%, without 6% |
| 2 | CreditCard_Total_Spending_0vs5_rel | Card spending, January vs. August | 16.2 | No spending 9%; if sharply reduced 37%; if maintained or increased, 43–47% |
| 3 | SavingAccount_Total_Amount_sum_u6m | Money moved through the savings account (6 months) | 8.7 | Rises from 18% (little) to 56% (most) |
| 4 | SavingAccount_Salary_Payment_Amount_sum_u6m | Salary received at the bank (6 months) | 7.5 | No salary 26%; higher salaries 75% |
| 5 | SavingAccount_Salary_Payment_Amount_0vs5_rel | Salary, January vs. August | 5.9 | Salary earners buy 59–72% |
| 6 | Operations_Bank_sum_u6m | Teller operations at the branch (6 months) | 5.0 | None 24%; 5 or more 57% |
| 7 | Client_Age_ord | Age range | 4.4 | Purchase rate rises with age |
| 8 | CreditCard_Total_Limit | Card limit (January) | 4.0 | |
| 9 | SavingAccount_Days_with_use_sum_u6m | Days with savings account activity | 3.5 | |
| 10 | SavingAccount_Balance_Average | Average balance (January) | 3.5 | |
| 11 | CreditCard_Spending_Aut_Debits_0vs5_rel | Automatic debits on card, January vs. August | 3.1 | |
| 12 | Month_Last_product_Months | Months since last product acquired | 2.5 | |
| 13 | SavingAccount_Active_ARG | Has a peso savings account | 2.4 | |
| 14 | Last_First_product_Months | Months between first and last product | 2.2 | |
| 15 | SavingAccount_Salary_Payment_Amount_0vs2_rel | Salary, January vs. November | 1.7 | |

**The buying customer, in Marketing's words:**

- Has a credit card and uses it, and hasn't reduced spending in recent months.
- Uses the account a lot: moves more money and has more days with activity.
- Gets their salary paid at the bank, especially a high one.
- Goes to the branch for teller operations.
- Is older (50 or more).
- Acquired a product recently: in decile 1, on average 8 months ago; in decile 5, 55 months ago.

The non-buyer is generally a "dormant" customer: no card, no salary at the bank and almost no activity.

**Careful when explaining:** importance is not causation. A card carrying a lot of weight doesn't mean giving someone a card will make them buy; it means people who have cards buy more.

Contacting only up to decile 7, the campaign earns about $506M versus $450M if everyone is contacted, with 7,000 fewer contacts and 98% of buyers captured.

**Assumptions** (illustrative, editable in cell 7.1): each contact costs $10,000 and each sale yields $100,000. With these numbers, contacting someone is worthwhile if they have more than a 10% chance of buying.

After decile 7 the result declines: deciles 8, 9 and 10 buy under 5%, so they cost more than they bring in. If the channel is more expensive, cut earlier (contact fewer deciles).

## 7. Likely questions and how to answer them

**Why LightGBM?** It builds many small trees that correct each other, works very well on tables with many variables, is fast, and shows which variables matter most.

**How do you know the model isn't overfitted?** Train AUC is 0.854 and test is 0.836: nearly the same. Also, hyperparameters were chosen with cross-validation inside train, without looking at test.

**Why 15 variables?** I tested 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 and 50 with cross-validation. Going from 10 to 15 improves; after that the gain is under 0.01 AUC. 15 is a good balance between performance and simplicity.

**Why did you recalculate target %?** Computing it on all customers is data leakage with a model: the variable already "knows" whether test customers bought. Recalculating on train only keeps the evaluation honest.

**Why the 99th percentile for outliers?** It is the most conservative: it only touches the top 1% and removes no customers. Outliers barely affect LightGBM because it splits by order, but they do distort the means and deviations in the aggregates.

**Isn't using March card type and region looking into the future?** In the history they were empty and there was no other source. They are nearly fixed customer attributes (each customer has one region), so the risk is low. I flag it as a limitation.

**And the last-product date?** The dataset runs to April 2019, but in the January snapshot I use, the maximum date is 01/31/2019. No leakage.

**What is lift?** How many times better than random. Lift 2.6 in decile 1 = those customers buy 2.6 times more than average.

**Why not use the 0.5 cutoff?** Marketing doesn't decide "yes or no" per customer: it decides how many to contact based on budget. Deciles give them that decision directly.

**Where do the $10,000 and $100,000 come from?** They are illustrative assumptions. They must be replaced with the product's real figures; the notebook has them as parameters.

**What would you do next?** Launch the campaign with a control group, measure the real result, re-score the base every month and retrain the model when performance drops.
